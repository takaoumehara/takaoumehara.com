Challenge 課題

Carnegie Foundation's NILS platform was struggling with user engagement. The PDSA (Plan-Do-Study-Act) cycle — the core methodology that improvement communities depended on — was inefficient and hard to navigate. Users found the interface overwhelming, with too many fields, unclear sequencing, and no sense of progress. The platform that was supposed to support continuous improvement was itself becoming an obstacle to improvement work. Teachers and administrators were abandoning the workflow mid-cycle, not because they didn't value the methodology, but because the tool made it feel like bureaucratic burden rather than reflective practice. Carnegie Foundation の NILS プラットフォームはユーザーエンゲージメントに苦戦していた。PDSA（Plan-Do-Study-Act）サイクル — 改善コミュニティが依存する中核的方法論 — は非効率でナビゲートが困難だった。ユーザーはインターフェースを圧倒的と感じ、フィールドが多すぎ、シーケンスが不明確で、進行の感覚がない。継続的改善をサポートするはずのプラットフォームが、改善作業の障害そのものになっていた。教師と管理者は方法論を評価しないからではなく、ツールが官僚的負担のように感じさせたため、ワークフローをサイクルの途中で放棄していた。

Solution 解決策

I started with interaction research: tested two competing concepts — vertical scrolling versus horizontal scrolling — to determine which pattern users found most intuitive for multi-step workflows. User testing validated vertical scrolling as the most familiar and least disorienting approach. From there, I redesigned the PLAN and DO phases with step-by-step guidance, progress indicators, and collaborative features. Each phase received clear instructions, document attachment capabilities, and commenting tools. The goal was to make the PDSA cycle feel like a guided conversation rather than a form to fill out. The redesign led to a 40% increase in platform adoption and 25% operational efficiency boost. インタラクション研究から始めた：2 つの競合するコンセプト — 縦スクロール対横スクロール — をテストして、ユーザーがマルチステップワークフローに最も直感的と感じるパターンを決定した。ユーザーテストは縦スクロールが最も馴染みがあり、最も混乱しないアプローチであることを検証した。そこから、ステップバイステップガイダンス、進行インジケーター、協力機能を備えた PLAN と DO フェーズを再設計した。各フェーズは明確な指示、ドキュメント添付機能、コメントツールを受け取った。目標は、PDSA サイクルを記入するフォームではなく、ガイド付きの会話のように感じさせることだった。再設計はプラットフォーム採用率 40% 増加と運用効率 25% 向上につながった。

The Research-First Approach

Before committing to any visual direction, I needed to validate the interaction model. The PDSA cycle is inherently sequential — Plan, then Do, then Study, then Act, then back to Plan — but that sequence could map to many different UI patterns: horizontal tabs, vertical steps, wizard flows, accordions. Rather than designing on instinct, I tested two interaction concepts — vertical scrolling versus horizontal scrolling — with real users. The research was clear: vertical scrolling was more intuitive, less disorienting, and felt more familiar to educators already accustomed to form-based tools. Only after that validation did I move into the redesign.

Design Strategy: Conversation, Not Form

The redesigned PLAN and DO phases each broke down into clear steps with progress indicators, so users always knew where they were in the cycle. Instructions were written in plain language, positioned at the top of each step. Document attachment and commenting tools were integrated inline — no secondary screens or modals to hunt for. The result was a workflow that felt like a guided conversation: "Tell us your goal. Now describe your approach. Attach evidence. Reflect on what happened." The same methodology, but the interface now supported reflection rather than demanding compliance.
